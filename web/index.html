Ear Number

Three digits that say how well one ear hears, and that turn back into a hearing chart. Higher is better: 999 is a healthy young ear and 000 is no hearing at all.

Where is your result from?

An audiogram plots the quietest sound you heard at each pitch. Pitch runs across the top; loudness runs down the side, with 0 at the top and bigger numbers, worse hearing, lower down. If you were not given a copy, ask the clinic for one.

  • Read the circles (O, usually red) for the right ear and the crosses (X, usually blue) for the left. Where a triangle (right) or square (left) appears at a pitch, use it instead: it is the same test with masking.
  • Ignore < > [ ]: they are bone conduction, a different test.
  • An arrow on a symbol means no response even at the loudest sound tested. Your threshold is somewhere beyond that level: enter the level shown, or a higher value if you prefer. Anything above 100 counts as 100.
  • Values at 250, 750 and 1500 Hz are not needed. You can also place each point by clicking the charts above.

Enter each value in dB HL, as printed. 3 kHz and 6 kHz are optional; leave them blank if your result does not have them. Many workplace tests stop at 6 kHz; if yours has no 8 kHz, the page will offer to copy the 6 kHz value.

A value marked as no response (often NR or an arrow) means you heard nothing even at the loudest level tested: enter that level, or a higher value if you prefer. Anything above 100 counts as 100.

In Apple Health, open Hearing, then Audiograms, and open the test to see the value for each ear at each pitch. Other apps usually show a similar chart or list.

Phones and earbuds are not calibrated like clinic equipment, so the numbers are marked E (estimated). Apple's test measures losses only up to about 85 dB HL: where a value sits at that limit, your hearing there may be worse, and you can enter a higher value if you choose.

About Ear Number

Why does hearing need a number?

Eyesight has "20/20". Hearing has the audiogram: a chart of the quietest sound an ear can detect at each of seven or so pitches. It describes an ear well, but nobody remembers theirs, and it is awkward to type into a device. "Mild" or "moderate" loss is too coarse to set anything up with and says nothing about which pitches are affected.

Ear Number is short enough to remember and quote, ordered so that a bigger number is better hearing, and detailed enough for a music player, earbuds or a hearing-aid app to turn back into a chart.

How do I read a number?

A person has two numbers, left ear first, then a check digit that lets software catch a mistyped number: 705 688 4. Each number answers two questions.

How much hearing. The scale runs in blocks of 50, and each block is a 5 dB step in the ear's average threshold, weighted towards the pitches that matter most for speech. Subtract the number from 1000 and divide by ten, and you have that average in dB to within 5 dB.

800–999under 20 dBnormal hearing
650–79920 to 35 dBmild loss
500–64935 to 50 dBmoderate loss

000–04995 dB or morelittle or no hearing

What kind. Within a block of 50, the position picks one of 50 typical shapes: even across the pitches, treble falling away early or late, a dip around 4 kHz, and so on. This part describes; it does not rank. 905 and 940 are equally good ears of different kinds, while 950 is one step better than 949.

  • When two numbers are 50 or more apart, the higher one is the better ear on this measure. Closer than that, read nothing into which is higher.
  • A hearing test is not perfectly repeatable, so neither is the number. Taking the test again often changes the last two digits and can move the number into the next block. Treat a change of 100 or more as real.

What do 000, E and N mean?
  • 000 means no hearing on that side, or no ear.
  • E, as in 456E, means the number came from a self-test on a phone or earbuds, not from a clinic. Such numbers are rougher.
  • N means that ear was not measured and its value is assumed, usually copied from the other ear.

Where do the numbers come from?

The table behind the number was learnt from the hearing tests of 29,061 ears, aged 6 to over 80, in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). On ears held back from building it, the chart decoded from the number differs from the measured one by about 5 dB, about as much as a second hearing test on the same ear.

What is it not?

Ear Number describes hearing thresholds and nothing else. It is not a diagnosis, says nothing about the cause of a hearing loss, and does not replace a hearing assessment. It does not capture how well someone follows speech in noise, how loud is too loud for them, or tinnitus, and it knows nothing below 500 Hz or above 8 kHz.

It is not the Hearing Number promoted by Johns Hopkins University, which is a single average in dB where lower is better. Ear Number's "how much" part is close to it: (1000 − Ear Number) / 10.

Is anything I enter sent anywhere?

Everything happens in your browser. Nothing you type or drag is sent anywhere. A copied link keeps the numbers after the #, a part of the address that browsers do not send to the server. The share links are plain links: nothing from those services loads on this page, and they receive only what you choose to post.
